How to Create an Invoice Online: What It Must Contain and How to Do It in Minutes

An invoice is more than a bill — it is the document that gets you paid. A clear, professional invoice tells your client exactly what they are paying for, how much, by when, and how to pay. A messy one invites questions, delays and disputes. Whether you are a freelancer, a small shop supplying goods, or a service provider billing monthly, this guide covers what every invoice must contain and how to create one online for free.

What a Professional Invoice Must Contain

Whatever country or industry you work in, a complete invoice includes these elements:

  • The word "Invoice" and a unique invoice number (for example INV-001). Numbers must never repeat — your records and your client's records both depend on them.
  • Your details: your name or business name, address, phone and email. Add your logo if you have one; it builds trust and looks professional.
  • Your client's details: the client or company name and address, and the name of your contact person if relevant.
  • Dates: the date the invoice is issued and the payment due date. "Due on receipt" or "Net 14" (payable within 14 days) are common terms — write them clearly rather than assuming.
  • An itemised list: each product or service on its own line, with a clear description, quantity, unit price and line total. "Services — 10,000" is weak; "Urdu-to-English translation, 5,000 words @ 2 per word" is clear.
  • Subtotal, taxes and total: show the subtotal, any tax you are registered to charge (such as GST/VAT or sales tax) as a separate line, any discount, and the final total amount due in bold.
  • Payment instructions: how the client should pay — bank account title and number, IBAN where relevant, or mobile payment details (for example JazzCash or EasyPaisa in Pakistan). Missing payment details are the most common cause of late payment.
  • Notes: a short thank-you, your terms (late fee policy if you have one), and any reference such as a purchase order number.

Tips for Freelancers and Small Businesses

  • Invoice immediately. Send the invoice the day work is delivered, not at month-end. The clock on your payment terms starts when the client receives it.
  • Keep a simple sequence. Number invoices in order (INV-001, INV-002…) and keep copies in one folder. When tax season or a client query arrives, you will thank yourself.
  • Agree terms before the work starts. Deposit percentage, due date and what is included — put them in your first email or agreement so the invoice holds no surprises.
  • Follow up politely and on time. A short reminder a day before the due date, and again a few days after, is normal business practice — most late payments are forgetfulness, not bad faith.
  • Match the invoice to the quotation. If you quoted a price, the invoice should mirror it line by line. Any change should be explained in the notes, not hidden in the total.

A Worked Example

Here is a complete invoice for a freelance designer, with every line doing its job:

  • Invoice INV-014, issued 3 November 2026, due 17 November 2026 (Net 14).
  • Logo and brand design — 1 project × PKR 60,000 = PKR 60,000.
  • Website copywriting — 10 pages × PKR 3,000 = PKR 30,000.
  • Subtotal: PKR 90,000. Tax: none (the designer is not registered to charge it, so no tax line is invented).
  • Deposit already paid: PKR 45,000 (the 50% agreed before work began). Balance due: PKR 45,000.
  • Payment instructions: bank transfer to the account named on the invoice — the client never has to ask "how do I pay you?"

Notice what the example avoids: no lump sum with no breakdown, no missing due date, and no tax figure the issuer has no right to charge. Each line can be checked by the client against the original quotation in seconds — which is exactly why invoices written this way get paid faster.

Common Mistakes

  • No due date, just "pay soon". Vague terms produce vague payment. A specific date turns your invoice into something a client can schedule.
  • Payment details left off. The most common cause of late payment is embarrassingly simple: the client was never told where to send the money.
  • Recycling an invoice number. Two invoices with the same number corrupt both your records and your client's, and look careless to any accountant who reviews them.
  • Editing an invoice after sending it. If something is wrong, cancel and reissue with a new number and a note. A silently changed invoice destroys trust when the two versions meet.
  • Descriptions only you understand. "Work — 25,000" forces the client to ask what they are paying for. Name the deliverable, the quantity and the rate.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between an invoice and a receipt?

An invoice is a request for payment, sent before you are paid. A receipt is proof of payment, sent after. Many small businesses send the invoice first and mark it "Paid" (or issue a receipt) once the money arrives.

Do I need to register a business to send an invoice?

In most places a freelancer can invoice under their own name; registering becomes necessary at certain income levels or for charging tax. Requirements differ by country, so check your local rules — but never let paperwork delay sending a clear invoice for work done.

Can I create an invoice on my phone?

Yes. A browser-based generator like the Loona Invoice Generator works on any phone — fill in the fields, download the invoice, and share it straight from your phone.

What if my client asks for changes after I sent the invoice?

Do not edit the sent invoice silently. Cancel it, issue a corrected one with the next invoice number, and note the change. A clean paper trail protects both sides.
